  Overview& Context  

The addendum is one step in an eight-step pipeline — and the only one that involves maths.


Every contractor on a ReelShort shoot moves through the same paperwork pipeline, tracked row by row in a crew batch sheet. Seven of the steps are "send and collect". The addendum is different: it turns negotiated terms and the production's time record into a pay calculation the producer signs.

On the reference production — a 10-day Texas shoot with 12-hour contracted days — 27 contractors across 7 departments went through this pipeline, and 21 of them needed an addendum. Each one was rebuilt by hand in Word and a personal spreadsheet.

   The Problem   

The hard part wasn't the document. It was everything feeding into it.


Each addendum looks like a one-page form. Underneath sit three messy inputs — a free-text deal memo, a timecard export and a location rule — that only someone who knew the rules could reconcile. I started from the real crew batch sheet and traced every number on a signed addendum back to its source.

Deals arrive as sentences
"$1000 flat prep, $350 / 12 hour shoot day, 10 days" had to be re-read and split into rate, hours, days and fees before any maths could start.
Overtime law differs by state
California pays 1.5× after 8 hours and 2× after 12; Texas and most other locations stop at 1.5×.

Fees compound on each other
Hourly rate is backed out of a weighted day; OT and meal penalties depend on it; agency fees apply to flat + base + OT, never to per-diems.
Batch work, one-off tools
Dozens of contractors paid in weekly waves — but addenda were built one at a time, with signatures pasted in after the fact.

   Design Principles   

Model the rules once. Let the interface stay simple.


Derive, never type
Hourly rate, OT, meal penalty, agency fee and total are outputs. Nobody hand-edits a number the engine owns.
The batch is the unit
Accountants think in productions and pay waves, so the home view is a project with progress and exposure.
Enforce, don't explain
Order, validation and approval locks live in the product, replacing tribal knowledge.


BEFORE
① Read the free-text deal memo
② Copy last project's Word template
③ Rebuild formulas in a spreadsheet
④ Look up the state's OT tier
⑤ Type figures, paste a signature, export
⑥ Repeat for ~21 contractors
AFTER · ADDENDUM STUDIO
① Pick the project — location rules apply
② Import the crew batch CSV
③ Fix only records flagged "Needs fix"
④ Finalize approval — record locks
⑤ Batch-export every addendum as PDF



   01 Rules Engine   

A rules engine, not a template.


Choosing a location sets the overtime bands for every record on the project; changing a day rate re-derives hourly rate, OT, meal penalty and agency fee instantly. Each fee category is an optional, collapsible section with a live subtotal — and blank categories disappear from the final document.






   02 Built for Batches   

The production, not the document, is the unit of work.


The workspace opens on crew count, finalized ratio, records needing attention and the running project total. The queue carries each contractor's amount and status, and filters straight to the records that block a pay wave. CSV import uses alias lists and fuzzy header matching, so the crew sheet production already keeps becomes the input.






   03 Guardrails   

Guardrails over manuals.


Crew editing stays locked until the project is defined. Validation runs on every keystroke and marks the exact field that's wrong. Export is blocked while any record fails. Once a producer finalizes, approver, date and signature lock — payment lines stay editable, but approval can't drift silently.




   04 WYSIWYG   

What you see is what gets signed.


The right-hand pane is the actual print layout, updating live. Reviewers never reconcile a form against a separate output — the document is the interface, and switching to a California unit re-renders every preview with its own overtime law.
