Where This Started

I was the user before I was the designer

Before designing the ReelShort Casting System, I spent a year on ReelShort's production operations team, managing onboarding, contracts and wrap across 40+ productions. Working with producers, casting directors and talent agencies every day showed me the workflows, frustrations and needs of the people I would later design for.

Two things from that year made this project possible.

First-hand knowledge of the workflow. I knew where casting decisions slowed down because I had been the one slowing down: opening a profile for a second photo, cross-checking an agency in a spreadsheet, finding a missing consent field after the call. These were problems I had met myself.

A network. The people I had worked with became the stakeholders, research participants and early adopters for the redesign. That is the main reason it could move as fast as it did.

The problem was not the database. It was everything around it.

The earlier casting system held talent records, but a decision still meant leaving it. A second photo lived on Instagram, availability lived in email, the agency relationship lived in someone's memory. Most of the team's time went into collecting information before a decision could begin.

The opportunity was to turn the system from a place where records are stored into a place where decisions are made.

The Problem

Leading the next evolution of the ReelShort Casting System

As ReelShort began exploring how AI and new technology could make production more efficient, improving internal tools became a priority. The casting team already had a basic casting system, but it worked like a database more than a tool for making decisions. I was asked to lead the redesign around two goals: fill the gaps in talent profiles, and make the platform more useful in the daily casting workflow.

Missing information

I audited existing profiles to find the fields casting teams rely on most and where the gaps were.

Workflow friction

I traced how producers search, compare and shortlist, and marked the repeated steps and needless navigation.

A better experience

I explored early Figma concepts that brought key talent information into the grid, so teams could evaluate actors without opening every profile.

The direction that came out of this: less time searching for information, more time making casting decisions.

User Research

What casting teams told me

Before committing to a direction, I interviewed five producers and casting directors, 30 minutes each, through my studio connections. We covered how they source talent, how they compare two candidates, and what makes them stop and open a profile. Four needs came up again and again.

01Visual fit

Multiple angles and looks, not a single headshot. The face is always the first filter.

Read the quote
“When we and the client choose the talent, their profile picture is always the most important first impression. You immediately know whether the talent's look meets the character's needs or whether they should go into the candidate pool. The profile photo is their kind of resume.”

02Complete information

Height, consent limits, agency, location. A profile missing any of these is skipped rather than investigated.

Read the quote
“When we select some talents and want to move forward, we spend a long time checking whether their schedule, location and consent fit the production. A complete profile with clear information right in our hand saves tons of time. Every piece of information matters in choosing the right person.”

03Chemistry

Casting happens in pairs and ensembles. Teams want to see two faces side by side.

Read the quote
“For a vertical romance drama, the chemistry between the two leads is everything. It's one of the biggest things that can make a show go viral. Sometimes two actors are perfect for their roles separately, but together the chemistry just isn't there. It's whether they work as a couple.”

04Agency network

The deal is with the agency. Who represents whom, and who actually responds, shapes the shortlist.

Read the quote
“Agencies usually hold a lot of talent, and it's much easier to book several talents from the same agency together. I would love to see the agency info when I look at a talent's profile.”
Key Design Decisions

Five decisions, prioritized against four needs and two sprints

The research produced a longer list of problems than two sprints could absorb. I weighed the four needs against the engineering budget and committed to five changes for phase one. The project manager approved the scope.

01 · Make Talent Card More Visual

The problem

Each card showed one photo, so teams had to open the full profile to see more of an actor. Too many tags cluttered the card and buried the information that mattered.

What changed

A photo carousel lets users browse several photos without opening a profile. I cut the tags back to key consent information, in simple color-coded labels.

Why this

Casting teams scan dozens of faces at once, and extra tags get in the way. Putting the focus back on the actor makes looks easier to compare and keeps browsing uninterrupted.

02 · Surface Information Completeness

The problem

A promising actor is not necessarily a casting-ready actor. Many profiles were submitted with missing details, and the team often found out late. There was no way to tell which profiles were complete without opening them one by one.

What changed

I identified the 12 data points casting teams need most and added a Profile Completeness indicator to every card, plus filters to sort high- and low-completeness profiles.

Why this

Making completeness visible up front means less time checking for gaps and more time evaluating talent. It also shows which profiles need attention before anyone moves forward.

03 · Rebuild Agency Library Page

The problem

Every cell was editable, long values were truncated, status was shown with emoji, and a destructive action sat on every row. The agency list and the talent library behaved like two separate databases.

What changed

One Add Agency flow with required fields. Each row now shows office locations and links to website, Instagram and LinkedIn, and emoji status became color-coded labels. Actor photo stacks with a count show who each agency represents and open the full roster in one click.

Why this

Teams need to know who an agency represents and whether they have worked together before, without digging through pages. The new layout puts that in one place and lowers the risk of accidental edits and deletions.

04 · Change the Filter's Information Architecture

The problem

Six rows and thirteen controls, all single-select. Users could not view two genders or two age ranges together. The logic was unclear and the emoji added noise.

What changed

I reorganized the hierarchy, merged overlapping criteria, removed unneeded options and introduced multi-select filtering.

Why this

Teams often explore several options at once, so multi-select makes search more flexible. A simpler structure makes each option easier to find, understand and use.

05 · Update VFX Information UI System

The problem

The VFX grading standards, D to S+, filled a full-width table. Users scrolled past it to reach the project list even when they did not need the grades.

What changed

I turned the table into a collapsible card with color-coded grade labels, a cleaner layout and a short pricing note, and moved the project list near the top. The full standards stay one click away.

Why this

Most users come to check projects, not read grading standards. The information stays reachable without getting in the way of daily tasks.

What we tested, what we put on hold

Not every idea made it into the first release. Two looked promising but ran into practical limits.

AI-powered talent attributes

We tried detecting hair and eye color from uploaded photos. Results were not reliable across lighting, editing and photo quality, so we held the feature rather than show inaccurate data.

IMDb and Instagram integration

I explored pulling photos and profile details from both to cut manual updates. API access and platform data restrictions made it impractical, so the integration was postponed.

Shipping and next steps

Phase one went live for the internal team, followed by a 20-minute walkthrough to help people get started. Availability syncing and side-by-side talent comparison moved to the phase-two backlog.

Reflection

Casting looks like choosing faces. Most of it is managing information, and the old tool made the team pay for the same information twice: once to find it, once to re-verify it. The redesign did not replace anyone's judgment. It removed the second payment.

The year on the production side turned out to be the method. I did not have to guess which field mattered or which stakeholder to convince. I had been the user, I knew the people, and that is where the design started.

The biggest thing I learned: good design is not always about adding features. Sometimes it is removing the friction that gets in the way of people doing their jobs.
